The one idea

Every Japanese verb form is a stem plus an ending. Nothing is memorised whole — you build it.

What separates the classes is only how many stems they have:

  • Ichidan verbs have one stem. Drop る, attach anything.
  • Godan verbs have five, one per vowel row. Pick the row the ending wants.
  • する and くる change their own vowel too, and are the only two that do.

So learning verbs is really two jobs: work out which class a verb is in, then learn which stem each ending attaches to. There is no third job.

Which class is a verb in?

Only verbs ending in る are ever ambiguous. Everything else is decided for you.

  • Doesn't end in る → godan. No exceptions. (のむ, かく, はなす, まつ, あそぶ)
  • Ends in る, and the sound before it is a, u, or o → godan. No exceptions. (つくる, ある, とる)
  • Ends in る, and the sound before it is i or e → usually ichidan, and this is the only case worth memorising. (たべる, みる, おきる)

The -iru / -eru verbs that are godan anyway

Short list, and it's worth knowing cold — these are common verbs.

VerbReadingMeaning

A quick way to check one you're unsure of: put it in the negative. Ichidan drops る（たべる → たべない）; godan shifts to the a-row and keeps the consonant（はしる → はしらない）. If はしらない sounds right and はしない sounds wrong, it's godan.

Two verbs read いる

いる（居る）— to be, for people and animals — is ichidan: いない, います.
いる（要る）— to need — is godan: いらない, いります.

The five stems

A godan verb shifts its final syllable through the five vowel rows. That's the whole system, and the name 五ご段だん means "five levels."

Take いく:

RowStemEnding it takesResult
aいかない, れる, せるいかない = don't go
iいきます, たい, ながらいきます, いきたい
uいく— (dictionary form)いく = go
eいけば, る, (command)いけば = if I go, いける = can go
oいこういこう = let's go

のむ behaves identically: のまない, のみます, のむ, のめば, のもう.

Ichidan verbs skip all of this. たべる drops る and every ending attaches to たべ: たべない, たべます, たべれば, たべよう.

The one sound rule

Godan verbs ending in う use わ in the a-row, not あ — the plain あ sound was lost here centuries ago.

✕かあない

✓かわない　(かう = to buy)

Likewise うたう → うたわない, いう → いわない.

The て-form

The て-form is the one place godan verbs don't simply follow the vowel rows, because the sounds wore down over time. It's five groups, sorted by the last kana of the dictionary form.

Dictionary ends inBecomesExample
う・つ・るってかう → かって
まつ → まって
とる → とって
む・ぶ・ぬんでのむ → のんで
あそぶ → あそんで
しぬ → しんで
くいてかく → かいて
ぐいでおよぐ → およいで
すしてはなす → はなして

Ichidan needs no rule at all — drop る, add て: たべる → たべて, みる → みて.

And the two irregulars: する → して, くる → きて.

いく is the one exception

By the く rule it should be いいて. It isn't.

✕いいて

✓いって

This is the only verb in the language that breaks the て-form rules.

The た-form is free

Here's the part worth knowing before you memorise anything else: the past tense uses exactly the same rule as the て-form. Swap て for た, で for だ. That's it.

Verbて-formた-form (past)
かうかってかった
のむのんでのんだ
かくかいてかいた
およぐおよいでおよいだ
はなすはなしてはなした
たべるたべてたべた
いくいっていった

So the five groups above are not a て-form rule — they're a rule you use twice. Learn them once and the past tense costs you nothing.

It pays a third time, too: たら conditionals are just the た-form plus ら（いったら = if I go）, and たり is the た-form plus り.

On たべれる

たべられる is the textbook potential. たべれる — the ら-nuki or "ら-dropped" form — is what most people actually say. Both are understood; られる is the safer choice in writing or with someone senior to you.

Plain and polite

Every verb has four basic forms, and each exists twice — once plain, once polite. Eight cells, and you already have the pieces for all of them.

PlainPolite
Presentいくいきます
Negativeいかないいきません
Pastいったいきました
Negative pastいかなかったいきませんでした

The polite column is entirely regular: it's the i-stem plus ます, ません, ました, ませんでした. No sound changes, ever. Even いく — the verb that breaks the て-form — is perfectly regular here: いきます.

Where いかなかった comes from

Look at the plain negative past. It isn't a new ending — ない is itself an い-adjective, so it conjugates like one: ない → なかった, exactly as さむい → さむかった.

That means the negative of a verb behaves like an adjective for the rest of its life:

  • いかない → いかなかった　(didn't go)
  • いかない → いかなくて　(て-form)
  • いかない → いかなければ　(ば-conditional)

たい works the same way — いきたい is an い-adjective too: いきたかった, いきたくない, いきたくなかった.

What the て-form unlocks

The て-form isn't a tense. It's a connector — it hands the verb to something else, which is why it's worth the effort of learning the sound changes.

PatternMeansExample
て + いるongoing, or a resulting stateたべている = is eating
しっている = knows
て + くださいplease doまってください = please wait
て + もいいmay, is allowed toいってもいい = may go
て + はいけないmust notいってはいけない = must not go
て + からafter doingたべてから = after eating
て + みるtry doingたべてみる = try eating
て + しまうdo completely, or regrettablyたべてしまった = ate it all / ate it, oops
て + おくdo in advanceかっておく = buy it ahead of time
て, て, て…and then — joins clausesおきて、たべて、いった

ている is the one to get right early. With action verbs it's a progressive（たべている = is eating）, but with verbs describing a change it's a resulting state, not an action in progress.

✕しっています meaning "am knowing"

✓しっています = know　(the knowing already happened; you are in the state of knowing)

✕けっこんしています meaning "am getting married"

✓けっこんしています = am married　(the state, not the ceremony)

This also explains a form that surprises everyone: "I don't know" is しりません／しらない, never ✕しっていません. The positive しっている is a state, but Japanese drops back to the plain negative of しる to say you are not in it. しりません is the polite default; しらないです exists and is fine in casual-polite speech, just less polished.

Potential, volitional, imperative

Three forms that come straight off the stems, with no sound changes to learn.

Potential verbs are themselves ichidan, so they keep conjugating: いける → いけない, いけた, いけて.

Passive and causative

Both attach to the a-stem for godan verbs, and both produce new ichidan verbs — so everything you already know keeps applying to the result.

Passive — was done to me

ClassRuleExample
Godana-stem + れるいう → いわれる = be told
Ichidandrop る + られるたべる → たべられる = be eaten
するirregularされる
くるirregularこられる

Japanese also uses the passive where English wouldn't — to say something happened to your detriment: あめにふられた, literally "I was rained on."

Causative — make or let someone do

ClassRuleExample
Godana-stem + せるいく → いかせる = make/let go
Ichidandrop る + させるたべる → たべさせる
するirregularさせる
くるirregularこさせる

Whether it means make or let comes from context and particle: を tends toward making someone, に toward letting them.

Causative-passive — was made to do

Stack them: causative, then passive. いかせる → いかせられる = was made to go.

Godan verbs usually contract this to される: いかされる.

✕はなさされる

✓はなさせられる　(す-verbs never contract — it would collide with the passive)

られる is doing three jobs

For ichidan verbs, たべられる can mean can eat, is eaten, or an honorific eats. Only context separates them.

This is a large part of why the ら-nuki potential — たべれる — spread in speech: it frees られる up for the other two.

Four ways to say "if"

All four translate as "if" or "when," and they are not interchangeable.

FormBuilt fromUse it for
とplain form + とautomatic results. Always true, no exceptions, no intention.
はるになるとさくらがさく
ばe-stem + ば　(ichidan: drop る + れば)general or hypothetical conditions.
やすければかう
たらた-form + らa specific case, or "once X happens."
うちにかえったらでんわします
ならplain form + ならgiven what you just told me.
いくなら、かさをもっていって

たら is the safest default when you're unsure — it accepts the widest range of situations.

Adjective and noun conditionals（さむければ, しずかなら, がくせいなら）are on the reference page.

と is the one that gets misused. It can't be followed by a request, a command, or an intention, because it describes something automatic.

✕ひまだと、えいがをみましょう

✓ひまだったら、えいがをみましょう　(an invitation needs たら, not と)

なら is the only one that can attach to something the other person just said — which is why it so often opens a reply.

Adjectives conjugate too

Japanese adjectives carry tense, which English ones don't. さむかった is a single word meaning "was cold" — no verb involved.

Which pattern a word follows depends on its class: い-adjectives conjugate like verbs, な-adjectives conjugate like nouns — they borrow だ／です to do it.

い-adjectives — さむい

PlainPolite
Presentさむいさむいです
Negativeさむくないさむくないです　/　さむくありません
Pastさむかったさむかったです
Negative pastさむくなかったさむくなかったです　/　さむくありませんでした

Notice the polite column never changes the word — です is bolted on afterwards, because さむい already carries the tense itself.

✕さむいでした

✓さむかったです　(the adjective takes the past tense, not です)

な-adjectives — しずか

PlainPolite
Presentしずかだしずかです
Negativeしずかじゃないしずかじゃないです　/　しずかじゃありません
Pastしずかだったしずかでした
Negative pastしずかじゃなかったしずかじゃなかったです

Identical to how a noun behaves: がくせいだ, がくせいじゃない, がくせいだった. That's the whole point of the class.

The other forms, side by side

Jobい-adjectiveな-adjective

One pattern runs through that entire table: い-adjectives drop the い, な-adjectives use the bare stem. さむい loses its い to become さむく, さむくて, さむすぎる, さむそう. しずか simply takes に, で, すぎる, そう as it is.

✕はしない

✕あらない

✕しるません

The ある one catches everyone. ある is godan and conjugates normally everywhere else — あります, あって, あった — but its negative is suppletive: it's simply ない.

Why the て-form changed

Optional, but it turns an arbitrary table into three sound changes you can hear.

Originally every godan verb formed て off the i-stem, exactly like ます does — かき + て, のみ + て, かい + て. Then fast speech wore the middle down. Japanese calls this 音おん便びん, "sound convenience."

ChangeWhat happenedResult
促そく音おん便びんthe syllable collapsed into a glottal stop っまち + て → まって
撥はつ音おん便びんthe syllable nasalised into ん, voicing the て to でのみ + て → のんで
イ音おん便びんthe consonant dropped, leaving the い behindかき + て → かいて

う-verbs did the same thing by a longer road: かう was once かふ, so かひ + て → かうて → かって. That is why かう and まつ and とる all land on って.

す verbs never changed because し + て was already easy to say — which is why はなして looks so regular next to the others.

It also explains the voicing. で and だ appear exactly where ん appears, because a nasal voices the consonant after it. のんで, あそんで, しんで — and およいで, where the ぐ left its voicing behind as it dropped.

The same wearing-down produced さむかった from さむく + あった, which is why adjectives and verbs both have a past tense full of small っ.
