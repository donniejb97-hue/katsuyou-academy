Understanding Conjugation

How Japanese Verbs Work

A clear, visual guide to understanding Japanese verb conjugation patterns. Learn the logic behind the forms so you can conjugate any verb with confidence.

Why Conjugation Matters

In Japanese, verbs change their form to express time (past/present), politeness, negation, desire, ability, and more. Unlike English where we add helper words ("I will go", "I can go"), Japanese transforms the verb itself.

Example:
食べる (taberu) = to eat
食べない (tabenai) = don't eat
食べます (tabemasu) = eat (polite)
食べた (tabeta) = ate
食べたい (tabetai) = want to eat

The Three Verb Types

Every Japanese verb falls into one of three classes. The first two hold thousands of verbs each. The third holds exactly two — and that is the whole irregular verb system of the language.

五段 Godan

"Five-step" verbs that conjugate by shifting through 5 vowel rows

Examples:
行く、話す、飲む

一段 Ichidan

"One-step" verbs with a stem that never changes

Examples:
食べる、見る、寝る

不規則 Irregular

Only two verbs in the entire language — they change their own vowel as well as their ending

Examples:
する (to do)、来る／くる (to come)
Nouns + する（勉強する、料理する）are not a fourth class — they are just する with a noun bolted on, and conjugate exactly like it.

How to Identify Verb Types

Step 1: Is it する or くる?

✓
YES → It's irregular
する、来る（くる）— and any noun + する: 勉強する、散歩する、料理する
来る is easy to miss. It ends in る with a u sound before it, so every rule below would send it to godan. It isn't. Learn it here, with する, and the rest of the flowchart stays honest.
→
NO → Continue to Step 2

Step 2: Does it end in る — and is the sound before the る an i or an e?

✓
YES → Probably Ichidan
食べる (taberu), 見る (miru), 起きる (okiru), 寝る (neru)
It's the vowel that matters, not the kana. 起きる ends in きる, not いる — but the ki carries an i, so it qualifies. If the sound before る is a, u or o（つくる、ある、とる）, it's godan with no exceptions at all.
⚠️ Watch Out for Exceptions!
These end in -iru or -eru but are godan. It's a short list and worth knowing cold:
走る (hashiru) — to run
知る (shiru) — to know
入る (hairu) — to enter
帰る (kaeru) — to return home
切る (kiru) — to cut
要る (iru) — to need
減る (heru) — to decrease
しゃべる (shaberu) — to chat
A test for one you're unsure of: try the negative. Ichidan drops る（食べる → 食べない）; godan shifts to the あ-row（走る → 走らない）. If 走らない sounds right and ✕走ない sounds wrong, it's godan.
Two verbs read いる: 居る (to be, for people and animals) is ichidan — いない, います. 要る (to need) is godan — いらない, いります.

Step 3: Everything else

✓
It's Godan
Ends in: く、ぐ、す、つ、ぬ、ぶ、む、る、う
Examples: 行く (iku), 話す (hanasu), 飲む (nomu), 買う (kau)
If it doesn't end in る, it's godan. No exceptions. That covers most of the language, which is why the only thing worth memorising is the short -iru／-eru list above.

Foundation: The Hiragana Sound System

Japanese sounds are organized in rows and columns. Godan verbs work by shifting between these rows:

Row k- g- s- t- n- b- m- r- w-
あ (A) か が さ た な ば ま ら わ
い (I) き ぎ し ち に び み り い
う (U) く ぐ す つ ぬ ぶ む る う
え (E) け げ せ て ね べ め れ え
お (O) こ ご そ と の ぼ も ろ お
⚠️ The one sound rule: う-verbs take わ
Verbs ending in う use わ in the あ-row, not あ — the plain あ sound was lost here centuries ago.
✕ かあない　→　✓ かわない（買う = to buy）
Likewise うたう → うたわない、いう → いわない.
💡 Key Insight:
Notice how each column keeps the same consonant sound (k-, g-, s-, t-, n-, b-, m-, r-, w-), but the vowel changes (a, i, u, e, o). When we conjugate godan verbs, we're moving up and down these columns to create different forms.
Every godan ending is somewhere in this grid: く／ぐ in the k- and g- columns, す in s-, つ in t-, ぬ in n-, ぶ in b-, む in m-, る in r-, and う in w-. That last one has a catch — see below.

Godan Verbs: The Five-Row System

Godan verbs work by changing the final sound to a different row. Let's use 話す (hanasu - to speak) as our example:

Watch the Transformation

NEGATIVE (あ)
話さない
hanasanai
ます-STEM (い)
話します
hanashimasu
DICTIONARY (う)
話す
hanasu
POTENTIAL (え)
話せる
hanaseru
VOLITIONAL (お)
話そう
hanasou

Step-by-Step Process

1. Start with the dictionary form: 話す (hanasu)
2. Identify the final sound: す (su) - this is in the う-row
3. Find it in the chart: Look in the s-column, う-row = す
4. To make negative: Move to the あ-row in the same column: す → さ
5. Add the ending: はなさ + ない = 話さない

⚠️ The sound-change table: て-form and past tense

Two forms don't follow the row-shift pattern: the て-form and the past (た-form). The good news is that they use the same table. Learn it once for て, then swap て → た and で → だ, and the past tense costs you nothing.

Ends in て-form Past (た) Example
う・つ・る って った 買う → 買って／買った
む・ぶ・ぬ んで んだ 飲む → 飲んで／飲んだ
く いて いた 書く → 書いて／書いた
ぐ いで いだ 泳ぐ → 泳いで／泳いだ
す して した 話す → 話して／話した
行く is the one exception in the entire language
By the く rule it should be ✕行いて. It isn't — it's ✓行って／行った. No other verb breaks this table.
Ichidan verbs need no rule at all: drop る, add て or た（食べる → 食べて／食べた）. The irregulars are する → して／した and 来る → 来て／来た.

Ichidan Verbs: The Simple Pattern

Ichidan verbs are called "one-step" because the stem never changes. You simply drop る and add endings.

The Formula
Stem + る → Stem + ending

Example: 食べる (taberu - to eat)

Form Process Result
Negative たべ + ない 食べない
Polite たべ + ます 食べます
Past たべ + た 食べた
Want to たべ + たい 食べたい
Potential たべ + られる 食べられる
Volitional たべ + よう 食べよう
💡 Why It's Easy:
With ichidan verbs, once you memorize the endings (ない, ます, た, たい, られる, よう), you can conjugate any ichidan verb. No row-shifting and no sound changes — the て-form and the past are as plain as everything else.
The only thing to stay alert to is which verbs are actually ichidan: 走る and 帰る look the part and aren't (see Step 2). And in speech you'll hear the potential shortened — 食べられる → 食べれる. Both are understood; られる is the safer choice in writing and on the JLPT.

The Two Irregular Verbs: する and 来る

These are the only two irregular verbs in Japanese. They change their own vowel, not just their ending, which is why no rule predicts them — but there are only two, and everything built on them inherits the same pattern. する also turns nouns into verbs（勉強 → 勉強する）, and those compounds conjugate exactly like plain する.

Example: 勉強する (benkyou suru - to study)

Form する becomes... Result
Negative しない 勉強しない
Polite します 勉強します
Past した 勉強した
Want to したい 勉強したい
Potential できる 勉強できる
Volitional しよう 勉強しよう

The other one: 来る (kuru - to come)

The kanji stays put while the reading changes underneath it — こ, き, く. Worth learning by sound, not by sight.

Form Result Reading
Dictionary来るくる
Negative来ないこない
Polite来ますきます
Past来たきた
て-form来てきて
Want to来たいきたい
Potential来られるこられる
Volitional来ようこよう
💡 Special Note:
Notice that する's potential is できる — a different word entirely, not a modified ending. That, plus the vowel changes in 来る, is what "irregular" means here. Everything else in Japanese is predictable from a rule.
